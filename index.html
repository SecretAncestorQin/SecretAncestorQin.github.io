戰國　WARRING STATES

Season 1 · Episode 1

Three families carve up Jin

Jin was the strongest state in northern China. Its rulers lost it without a foreign invasion: their own ministers took it apart, and the king of all China signed off on the theft.

c. 500 BCEthe setting

A duke with no power

China was nominally one kingdom under the Zhou king, but real power sat with regional states. In Jin, power had slipped a level further down. The duke reigned, and a few great ministerial families ran the armies and the land. After decades of feuding, four families were left: Zhi, Zhao, Han and Wei. Zhi was the strongest.

455 BCEthe demand

Zhi Yao asks for land

The head of the Zhi family, Zhi Yao, demanded territory from each of the other three, in the duke's name. Han and Wei were afraid and paid. Zhao Xiangzi, head of the Zhao family, refused. Zhi Yao marched on him and forced Han and Wei to join the attack.

455–453 BCEthe siege

The flood at Jinyang

Zhao Xiangzi fell back to the walled city of Jinyang and held it for about two years. Zhi Yao dammed a river and flooded the city. With the defenders close to starving, Zhao sent an envoy out at night to the Han and Wei camps with one argument: when Zhao is gone, you are next.

They agreed. On the chosen night the three families broke the dam the other way and turned the water onto the Zhi camp. Zhi Yao was killed and his family wiped out.

403 BCEthe recognition

The king makes it legal

Zhao, Han and Wei divided the Zhi lands, then most of what the duke still held. For fifty years they were rulers in fact and ministers in name. In 403 BCE King Weilie of Zhou ended the pretence and formally raised the three family heads to the rank of regional lord, equal to the duke they had robbed.

By the traditional count, the last scraps of Jin were divided in 376 BCE and the state disappeared.

Why it mattersthe turning point

The rules stop applying

Three of the seven great powers of the next era, Han, Zhao and Wei, were born here. The historian Sima Guang, writing 1,400 years later, chose this year to open his chronicle of China. His reason was that the king himself had rewarded men for overthrowing their lord. After that, nothing but force decided who ruled.

Before you start

Saying the names

Chinese names are written here in pinyin, the standard romanisation. Four letters trip up most English speakers.

Q

Like ch in “cheese”. Qin sounds like “chin”.

X

Like sh in “she”. Xianyang starts “shyen”.

Zh

Like j in “judge”. Zhao sounds like “jow”.

C

Like ts in “cats”. Cao sounds like “tsow”.

Family names come first. In Zhao Xiangzi, Zhao is the family. Rulers are usually known by titles given after death, such as King Weilie, so the name you read was never used to their face.
