戰國　WARRING STATES

Part 1 · Background

China before the empire

Historians call everything before 221 BCE the pre-Qin period. It covers about eighteen centuries. Here is the short version, so the Warring States make sense when you reach them.

c. 2070–1600 BCEXia 夏

A dynasty on the edge of legend

Later Chinese texts name the Xia as the first dynasty, founded by Yu, who tamed the floods. No writing from the period survives. Many archaeologists link the Xia to the Bronze Age site of Erlitou on the Yellow River, though the match is still argued over. The dates are traditional.

c. 1600–1046 BCEShang 商

Bronze and the first writing

The Shang are the first dynasty confirmed by their own records. Their kings asked questions of their ancestors by cracking ox bones and turtle shells with heat, then carved the questions and answers onto them. These oracle bones are the oldest known Chinese writing, and the script is a direct ancestor of the one used today. Shang workshops cast ritual bronze vessels of a quality nobody else in the world matched at the time.

1046–771 BCEWestern Zhou 西周

The Mandate of Heaven

The Zhou, a people from the west, overthrew the Shang. To justify it they offered an idea that shaped Chinese politics for three thousand years: Heaven gives the right to rule to a virtuous house and takes it away from a corrupt one. The Zhou kings could not govern so much land directly, so they handed out territories to relatives and allies, who ruled as hereditary lords and owed the king loyalty and troops.

770–c. 453 BCESpring and Autumn 春秋

The king fades, the lords compete

In 771 BCE invaders sacked the Zhou capital. The court moved east to Luoyi, modern Luoyang, and never recovered its strength. The king kept his title and his rituals. Real power passed to the regional states, which numbered well over a hundred at the start and swallowed one another steadily. The strongest lords took turns acting as protector of the realm. Confucius lived at the end of this period and spent his life arguing for a return to the old order.

c. 453–221 BCEWarring States 战国

Seven powers, one survivor

By now even the lords were being overthrown by their own ministers. Jin, the strongest state in the north, was carved up by three of its own ministerial families, and in 403 BCE the Zhou king recognised them as lords. Read the full story. Seven large states were left, each with a king, a professional army and a bureaucracy. They fought for about two and a half centuries until Qin conquered the other six and founded the first empire in 221 BCE.

Historians date the start of the period differently: 475, 453 and 403 BCE are all in use. This site follows the story from the fall of the Zhi family in 453 to the royal recognition of 403.

Part 3 · The players

The seven states

Each of the seven had its own character, and the differences decided who survived. They are listed here starting with the eventual winner.

秦
Qin QínCapital: Xianyang

Far west, in the Wei River valley behind mountain passes

Other states looked down on Qin as half-barbarian. From 356 BCE the minister Shang Yang rebuilt it around two things, farming and war: land and rank went to men who took enemy heads, and the old nobility lost its privileges. The result was the most disciplined state of the age.

Conquered the other six between 230 and 221 BCE.

魏
Wei WèiCapital: Anyi, later Daliang

Centre, on both sides of the Yellow River

The first state to reform. Under Marquess Wen and his minister Li Kui, around 400 BCE, Wei had the best army and the best administrators in China. Its position was its weakness: enemies on every side. Two defeats by Qi, at Guiling and Maling, ended its lead.

Fell in 225 BCE when Qin flooded Daliang.

韩
Han HánCapital: Yangzhai, later Xinzheng

Centre, wedged between Qin, Wei and Chu

The smallest of the seven and the most exposed. Han was known for its crossbows and for the administrative methods of its minister Shen Buhai. The philosopher Han Fei, whose writing shaped Qin government, was a Han prince.

The first to fall, in 230 BCE.

赵
Zhao ZhàoCapital: Handan

North, facing the steppe

In 307 BCE King Wuling ordered his soldiers to dress like the nomads and fight from horseback, which gave Zhao the best cavalry in China. It became the one state able to meet Qin in open battle, until the disaster at Changping in 260 BCE destroyed its army.

Handan fell in 228 BCE. A last remnant held out until 222.

燕
Yan YānCapital: Ji, near modern Beijing

Far north-east

Remote and usually the weakest of the seven. Its great moment came in 284 BCE, when its general Yue Yi led a coalition that overran almost all of Qi. Yan is best remembered for sending the assassin Jing Ke against the king of Qin in 227 BCE.

Conquered in 222 BCE.

齐
Qi QíCapital: Linzi

East coast, the Shandong peninsula

The richest state, living on salt, fish, iron and trade. Its capital Linzi was among the largest cities in the world, and its Jixia Academy paid scholars of every school to come and argue. Qi never fully recovered from the invasion of 284 BCE and sat out the final wars.

Surrendered without a fight in 221 BCE, the last to go.

楚
Chu ChǔCapital: Ying, later Chen and Shouchun

South, along the Yangtze and Huai rivers

The largest state by far, with its own gods, music and poetry. The northern states treated it as foreign. Its territory was vast but its nobles kept power the kings could never break. Qin took the capital Ying in 278 BCE and Chu moved east. The poet Qu Yuan belongs to this state.

Conquered in 223 BCE by the Qin general Wang Jian.
